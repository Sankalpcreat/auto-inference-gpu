The Quiet Engineering Behind Fast Language Models

When people talk about large language models, they usually talk about training: the thousands of accelerators, the months of compute, the enormous datasets. Yet for most of a model's life, it is not being trained at all. It is being used. Every question a person types, every document a model summarizes, every line of code it suggests is an act of inference, and the economics of inference decide whether a model is practical or merely impressive.

Inference has two very different phases. The first is prefill. When a prompt arrives, the model reads every token of it at once. This is a large, regular computation: big matrices multiplied by big matrices. Modern chips are extremely good at this kind of work, so prefill is usually limited by raw arithmetic throughput. A long prompt costs more, but the hardware stays busy and efficient.

The second phase is decode. After the prompt has been read, the model produces its answer one token at a time. To produce each new token, it must run through every layer of the network again, but this time for a single token. The arithmetic per step is tiny, yet the model still has to read all of its weights from memory. The result is that decode is limited not by arithmetic but by memory bandwidth: how many bytes per second can move from memory into the compute units. On many machines, a single user's decode leaves most of the arithmetic units idle, waiting for data.

This is why the size of a model's weights in bytes matters so much. A model stored with sixteen-bit numbers must move twice as many bytes per token as the same model stored with eight-bit numbers, and four times as many as a four-bit version. Quantization, the practice of storing weights with fewer bits, is therefore one of the most effective tools for speeding up decode on a single device. The cost is a small loss of precision, which careful methods keep within acceptable limits.

The second major consumer of memory traffic is the key-value cache. A transformer remembers what it has read by storing, for every token and every layer, a key vector and a value vector. During decode, each new token attends to all of these stored vectors. As a conversation grows longer, the cache grows with it, and reading the cache becomes a larger share of each step. Some newer architectures reduce this cost by replacing most attention layers with recurrent layers that keep a fixed-size state instead of a growing cache. A model that alternates three recurrent layers with one attention layer, for example, only stores a growing cache for a quarter of its layers.

Engineers also fight a third, less visible cost: overhead. Every operation on a graphics processor must be launched by the host, and each launch takes a few microseconds. A small model may run hundreds of tiny operations for every token it produces. When the operations themselves finish quickly, the launches dominate, and the processor spends much of its time waiting for instructions rather than doing work. Techniques such as fusing several operations into one kernel, or recording a whole step once and replaying it, exist mainly to remove this overhead.

In the spring of that year, a small research group ran a series of careful measurements on laptops with unified memory. The test lab's codename was BLUE HERON, and its goal was simple: measure how close real software came to the theoretical limit set by memory bandwidth. Their method was to divide the number of bytes the model must read per token by the measured time per token, and compare the result to the advertised bandwidth of the machine. They found that well-tuned software reached between sixty and eighty percent of the theoretical limit, while naive implementations sometimes reached less than thirty percent.

The group's most surprising finding concerned sampling. Choosing the next token is not free: methods that sort probabilities or draw random numbers add work to every step. For the smallest models they tested, this sampling work was a measurable fraction of the total time per token. For larger models it disappeared into the noise, because reading the weights took so much longer.

They also tested speculative decoding, a technique in which a cheap method guesses several future tokens and the full model checks all of the guesses in a single pass. When the guesses are right, several tokens are produced for the price of one decode step. When they are wrong, the extra work is wasted. The group reported that the technique helped most on predictable text such as code or repeated phrases, and least on creative writing, where the next word is genuinely hard to guess. The number of guessed tokens that were accepted on average, which they called the acceptance length, was 2.7 for code and only 1.3 for short stories.

Another lesson was about measurement itself. Laptops change their clock speed as they heat up, and background programs compete for memory bandwidth. A difference of two or three percent between two runs could easily be noise. The group therefore repeated every measurement several times, reported the median, and only trusted improvements that were larger than the variation they saw when repeating the same configuration. They also insisted on checking that answers stayed correct, because an optimization that makes the model faster by making it worse is not an optimization.

Finally, they warned against benchmarks that can be gamed. If a test measures speed over whatever the model happens to generate, then a change that makes the model stop earlier will look like a speedup even though the model did less work. A fair benchmark fixes the amount of work: the same prompts, the same number of generated tokens, and the same rules for every configuration. Only then do the numbers describe the system rather than the shortcut.

Their conclusion was modest. On a single device, the fastest inference comes from moving fewer bytes per token, removing per-step overhead, and avoiding work that does not change the answer. Most other tuning knobs, they found, moved results by less than the noise.
